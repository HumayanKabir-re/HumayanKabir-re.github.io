Symbolic Regression

Recovering the equation, not just the fit, in the AI Feynman tradition

In development July 2026 3 min read Design note

A design note. The approach and the reasoning behind it are described here; measured results are not, because I have not published a clean evaluation of this one yet.

Fitting versus explaining

Give a neural network a few thousand samples of a physical process and it will predict the next one accurately. Ask it what law governs the process and it has nothing to say. The knowledge is real and it is distributed across weights in a form no one can read.

Symbolic regression asks the harder question. Rather than a function that predicts well, it searches for the expression that generated the data: not a curve through the points but something like a closed form you could write on paper and check against a textbook.

The reference work here is AI Feynman, from MIT and Cornell among others, which recovered a large set of physics equations from data by exploiting structure that physical laws tend to have, such as symmetry, separability and dimensional consistency.

Why a transformer

Classical symbolic regression is a search problem, usually genetic programming over expression trees, and the search space grows brutally with expression length. It works and it is slow, and every new dataset restarts the search from nothing.

The alternative is to treat an expression as a sequence of tokens and learn to generate it. Train a transformer on millions of synthetic equations paired with sampled points, and at inference it proposes plausible expression structures directly instead of searching for them. What took a genetic search minutes becomes a forward pass.

Two details in that diagram carry most of the design.

The input is a set of points, not a sequence. Shuffling the samples must not change the answer, so the encoder cannot use positional encoding in the usual way.

And the decoder does not emit numbers. Asking a language model to produce 9.80665 as digits is asking it to do arithmetic it is bad at. Instead it emits the skeleton with placeholders, and the constants are fitted numerically by least squares afterwards. The model proposes the shape; optimisation decides whether the shape was right.

Synthetic and real data

Training needs millions of equation-and-samples pairs, which only synthetic generation can supply: sample a random expression tree from a grammar, evaluate it at random points, and use the pair. The risk is that the model learns the quirks of the generator rather than the structure of real laws, and then fails on anything a physicist would actually write.

That is why real data has to be in the evaluation even when it cannot be in the training set. A model that scores well on held-out synthetic expressions and cannot recover a known physical law has learned the generator, not the task.

The hard parts

Scoring is not obvious. Two expressions can be algebraically identical and textually different, so string comparison undercounts success. Numerical agreement across the sampled domain is more honest but accepts expressions that happen to match on the sample and diverge outside it.

Noise breaks exact recovery. Real measurements are noisy, and a search for the expression that reproduces the data exactly will fit the noise. Some complexity penalty is required, which reintroduces the question of how to trade accuracy against expression length.

Nothing guarantees uniqueness. Many expressions fit any finite sample. Recovering one that matches the data is not the same as recovering the one that generated it, and only the second is worth anything.

Where it stands

The approach above is what I built toward. What it does not yet have is the part that would make it worth reading: a clean evaluation on known equations with a scoring rule chosen in advance, run over multiple seeds. Until that exists this is a description of an approach rather than a result, and I would rather label it that way than quote a number I cannot reproduce.
