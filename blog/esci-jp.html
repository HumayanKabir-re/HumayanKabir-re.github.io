ESCI-JP

Toward open Japanese image data for product search

In development July 2026 4 min read Open Data
Background: the Mercato write-up Amazon ESCI

In development. There is no dataset to download yet, and this post is about what it needs to contain and why.

More data, weaker system

Building Mercato across English and Japanese produced a result I did not expect. The Japanese half of the benchmark is larger on every axis that usually matters, and it still gets the weaker search engine.

Japanese carries 10,407 queries against English's 8,956, 233,850 products against 164,900, and 297,883 graded judgments against 181,701. More queries, more products, more human labeling. The English system still retrieves better, because the thing Japanese is missing is not text.

Two datasets are in play here. Amazon's Shopping Queries Dataset supplies the queries, the products and the graded relevance labels, and it covers both locales evenly. SQID, a community extension, adds precomputed CLIP vectors for product images, and it covers the US locale only. No public dataset provides an equivalent for Japanese.

ESCI-JP is an attempt to fill that one cell.

What the gap costs

The missing images are not a rounding error in Mercato. English runs three retrieval channels in parallel: keyword matching, dense semantic search, and image similarity. Japanese runs two. Removing that third channel is measurable, because I measured it in the other direction while building the English side.

The loss propagates past retrieval. A learned reranker reorders whatever the retrievers return, and its features describe how each candidate scored in each channel. With no image channel there are no image features, so the Japanese model trains on eight where the English model has ten.

The Japanese reranker also improves its input less than the English one does, gaining 0.0121 NDCG@10 against 0.0319. I want to be careful about that pair of numbers. The two models differ in feature count and in the language they rank, and I have not run the ablation that would separate those, so the honest statement is that the gap is consistent with the missing features rather than caused by them.

There is a third cost that does not show up in any metric. Marketplace listings are written by sellers, and plenty of them are thin, keyword-stuffed, or simply wrong. In English a product whose text is useless can still be found by its picture. In Japanese there is nothing to fall back on, so those listings are effectively unreachable no matter how good the ranker gets.

Encoding, following SQID

The method is not novel and should not be. SQID already solved the hard part for English, and the useful thing to do is follow it closely enough that the two datasets can be used together. Each product image is passed through a CLIP encoder, and what gets published is the resulting vector plus a reference back to the original image.

Matching the embedding space matters more than it sounds. A vector is only comparable to other vectors from the same model, so a Japanese set encoded with a different CLIP variant would be unusable in the system that needs it. Whatever Mercato indexes English images with is what this has to use.

One practical lesson carried over from working with SQID: validate on ingest. Roughly 5% of its vectors are malformed, and they are the wrong shape rather than absent, which is the more annoying failure. A missing vector announces itself. A vector with the wrong dimensionality loads without complaint and fails somewhere deep in the index build. Checking dimensionality at load time and dropping the bad rows with a count is a few lines of code and saves an afternoon.

Table 1. One record per product image. Recording the model that produced each vector is what lets a consumer check compatibility instead of assuming it.

Why only vectors travel

Product photographs belong to the sellers and platforms that made them, and a dataset that redistributed them would not be distributable at all. Publishing embeddings and references sidesteps that. A vector is a derived representation, the reference points back at the original, and anyone who needs pixels fetches them from the source under whatever terms apply there.

Following SQID's precedent rather than improvising also means the licensing question has a known answer instead of a novel one. What I have not settled is the license the vectors themselves ship under, and how to handle products whose source images have moved or been removed by the time anyone reads the reference.

Where it stands

In development, and the honest summary is that the design is further along than the artifact. The next concrete step is encoding a first slice of the Japanese corpus and checking that those vectors behave in Mercato's index the way the English ones do, which is the smallest test that would tell me whether the rest is worth building.
